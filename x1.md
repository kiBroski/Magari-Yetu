Absolutely. For **this specific business**, I would set it up as a proper Kenyan technology/marketplace company from day one—not as a casual classifieds website.

Your existing Magariyetu concept is already much closer to a full marketplace: it has vehicles, trucks, machinery, tuk-tuks and spare parts, dealer storefronts, seller dashboards, verification, inspections, leads and paid promotion. 

Below is how I'd structure the business.

> **Important:** this is a practical business/legal roadmap, not a substitute for a Kenyan advocate or tax adviser. Before incorporation and especially before taking customer money, have an advocate review the final structure and contracts.

# 1. First: what exactly is the company?

I'd define the business as:

**A technology company operating a multi-vendor automotive marketplace in Kenya.**

The company itself **doesn't necessarily buy or sell the vehicles**.

Instead:

> Seller → lists vehicle/part/service → Buyer discovers it → Buyer contacts seller → transaction happens between them.

That distinction is extremely important.

Your company earns money from things like:

* Featured listings
* Dealer subscriptions
* Seller subscriptions
* Advertising
* Lead-generation services
* Dealer inventory management
* Verification/inspection services
* Premium analytics
* Parts/service advertising
* Potential financing/insurance referral commissions
* Potential transaction fees later

That means you're fundamentally a **marketplace/intermediary**, rather than a motor dealer.

# 2. Company structure I'd recommend

For this idea, I'd use:

### **Private company limited by shares**

For example:

**Magariyetu Technologies Limited**

or whatever final brand/company name you settle on.

Kenya's Companies Act allows a private company to be formed with one or more persons, and a private company must have at least one director; directors must be at least 18. ([Kenya Law][1])

The important thing is separating:

**Company ownership**

from

**Brand ownership**

from

**Platform ownership**

from

**individual seller/dealer ownership of their inventory.**

# 6. KRA / tax setup

The company needs its own tax identity and proper accounting.

KRA states that persons expecting to incur tax liabilities should register for a PIN, and companies have their own tax obligations. ([Kenya Revenue Authority][3])

You'll want your accountant to establish the applicable obligations, potentially including:

### Corporation tax

Tax on company profits.

### VAT

This becomes particularly relevant once taxable turnover reaches the applicable threshold. KRA currently states that persons supplying or expecting to supply taxable goods/services worth **KES 5 million or more annually** qualify for VAT registration, with voluntary registration also possible. ([Kenya Revenue Authority][4])

### PAYE

Once you employ people.

### Other payroll/statutory obligations

Depending on your employees and structure.

**Don't mix personal M-Pesa/bank accounts with company money.**

Open a company bank account and have marketplace revenue flow through the company.

# 7. The really important one: ODPC

This is a **big deal for your marketplace**.

You're going to collect things such as:

* Names
* Phone numbers
* Emails
* User accounts
* Seller information
* Dealer information
* Vehicle information
* Location
* Messages/inquiries
* Possibly ID documents
* Verification documents
* Payment information
* IP/device information
* Analytics

Your own architecture already anticipates phone OTP, user registration, inquiries, seller information and verification. 

So you need to treat data protection as a core business function—not a footer page.

The ODPC describes a **data controller** as the entity determining how personal data is used, while a **data processor** processes data on behalf of a controller. ([Data Protection Commissioner][5])

Your marketplace will likely be a **data controller** for much of its own user/customer processing.

You may also become a processor in particular arrangements.

---

# 8. ODPC registration

This is an area where I'd have your advocate/privacy consultant assess your exact circumstances before launch.

ODPC's current guidance says organizations processing personal data may need registration, with exemptions based on turnover/employees but important sector/activity exceptions. The ODPC specifically lists **direct marketing and transport**, among others, as activities for which the exemption doesn't apply. ([Data Protection Commissioner][6])

Given your marketplace involves automotive/transport-related activity and potentially direct marketing, **I would budget and plan for ODPC registration rather than assuming you're exempt.**

You'll also need:

* Privacy Policy
* Data retention policy
* Data breach procedure
* User consent mechanisms where appropriate
* Data access/deletion processes
* Data-subject rights procedures
* Vendor/processor agreements
* Security controls
* Access controls
* Proper handling of ID documents
* Rules for international/cloud data transfers

ODPC's registration portal specifically asks about personal data, sensitive data, data transfers and technical/organizational safeguards. ([Data Portal][7])

# 9. Your Terms & Conditions are extremely important

Your marketplace needs **two different relationships**.

### Relationship 1

**Marketplace ↔ Seller**

And:

### Relationship 2

**Marketplace ↔ Buyer**

Your Terms should clearly establish what you actually are.

Something along the lines of:

> The platform provides an online marketplace that enables independent sellers and buyers to discover and communicate with one another.

Then clearly state:

> Unless expressly stated otherwise, the platform is not the owner, seller, importer, manufacturer or agent of vehicles, spare parts or services listed by third parties.

That's important.

But don't use the Terms as an excuse to avoid responsibility for everything.

You still need proper consumer protection mechanisms.

* Fake mileage
* Fake vehicle descriptions
* Fake photos
* Fake documents
* Misrepresented ownership
* Fake dealers
* Fake spare parts
* Misleading prices
* "Accident free" claims without evidence
* Fake inspection badges
* Fake "duty paid" claims
* Counterfeit parts
* Misleading advertisements

This is where your existing trust architecture is actually very good.

You already distinguish:

**Verified Seller**

from

**Inspected Vehicle**

from

**Featured Listing**

Those should remain separate.

# 21. Do you need a Communications Authority licence?

This is an interesting one.

The Communications Authority's e-commerce development material has historically stated that online trade platforms are not themselves licensed under KICA merely because they operate an online marketplace. ([Communications Authority of Kenya][9])

However, CA's regulatory framework is evolving—the Authority currently lists revised KICA regulations from 2026. ([Communications Authority of Kenya][10])

So I would **not** simply assume:

> "We're an online marketplace, therefore no licence ever applies."

Have counsel check your exact activities before launch, especially if you later add:

* Communication services
* Wallets
* Payments
* Messaging infrastructure
* Delivery/logistics
* Other regulated services

You don't want to accidentally become a regulated operator because you expanded the business.

# 22. Competition law

As you grow, competition law becomes increasingly relevant.

The Competition Authority's mandate includes preventing misleading market conduct and protecting effective competition. ([cak.go.ke][11])

You should therefore avoid practices like:

> "Pay us and we'll mark your competitor's listings as fake."

or:

> "Dealers must use our inspection service or we will suppress them."

unless there's a legitimate, transparent reason.

Your marketplace rules should apply consistently.
